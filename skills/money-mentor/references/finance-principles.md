# Finance Principles Reference

This document synthesizes core principles from Ramsey (Baby Steps, debt snowball), Sethi (conscious spending, automation), Housel (psychology of money, patience), Bach (latte factor, automation), Collins (simple path to wealth), Clason (Richest Man in Babylon), and Warren (50/30/20).

## Analysis Checklist (Evaluate Every Position Review)

Use this checklist to systematically evaluate the household's financial health. Score each green/yellow/red and quantify the gap.

### Debt Strategy
- [ ] **Clarity on payoff method** — Is the household using snowball (psychological wins), avalanche (interest minimization), or hybrid (avalanche with psychological milestones)? Is the method documented?
- [ ] **Cascade logic** — Can you trace exactly which income dollars flow to which debts each month? Is the order intentional?
- [ ] **Interest arbitrage** — Is household paying >8% on debt while holding cash earning <3%? If yes, cash should flow to debt first.
- [ ] **Minimum payment discipline** — Are all minimums being paid on time? Any 30+ day lates?
- [ ] **Accelerator allocation** — When income rises or an unexpected check arrives, does it flow to the plan or leak to lifestyle?

### Cash Flow & Spending
- [ ] **Budget accuracy** — Does actual spending match the budget within ±5%? If not, why?
- [ ] **Category tracking** — Are the three buckets clear: needs (50%), wants (30%), debt/savings (20%)? Which bucket is leaking?
- [ ] **Fixed expense baseline** — What's the true minimum monthly spend to survive? (Mortgage/rent, utilities, food, transport, insurance)
- [ ] **Discretionary audit** — What subscriptions, services, or habits are optional?
- [ ] **Income volatility** — Is income stable or irregular? Is the budget built on conservative/optimistic/realistic income?

### Emergency Fund & Buffer
- [ ] **Fund size** — Is emergency fund 3-6 months of expenses? (Rule: smaller fund if stable job/dual income, larger if variable income or single earner)
- [ ] **Accessibility** — Is the fund in a liquid account (savings, not locked term deposit)?
- [ ] **Separation** — Is the emergency fund kept separate from the operating budget? (To prevent "planned" emergencies from derailing the plan)
- [ ] **Restoration rule** — If emergency fund is tapped, is there a plan to rebuild it before resuming debt acceleration?

### Insurance & Risk Management
- [ ] **Life insurance adequacy** — (See estate-principles.md for formula) Does coverage = 10-12x annual income minus existing assets?
- [ ] **Disability cover** — Can the household survive 3-6 months on one income if either earner becomes unable to work?
- [ ] **Property & liability** — Is home/vehicle insured? Is liability protection adequate (vehicle, home liability, umbrella)?
- [ ] **Income protection** — Is there a backup plan if the primary income earner loses their job?
- [ ] **Health insurance** — Are all household members covered? Is coverage adequate for major illness?

### Post-Debt & Wealth Building
- [ ] **Timeline clarity** — When will the household be debt-free? Is it tracked month-by-month?
- [ ] **Vision articulation** — What happens after debt is cleared? (Emergency fund boost? House upgrade? Retirement acceleration? This matters for motivation)
- [ ] **Tax-advantaged account usage** — Are the household's country's tax-advantaged retirement accounts being maximized? (401k, IRA, TFSA, ISA, superannuation)
- [ ] **Employer match capture** — If available, is the household getting the full employer match on retirement accounts?
- [ ] **Investment readiness** — Once debt is cleared, is there a written investment plan? (Or will there be decision paralysis?)

## Debt Attack Principles

### Snowball vs Avalanche vs Hybrid

**Snowball (Ramsey)**: Pay minimums on all debts, throw excess at the smallest balance.
- Pros: Quick wins (psychological), momentum, clarity
- Cons: Pays more interest overall, ignores rate differences
- Best for: High-spending households where momentum matters, multiple small debts

**Avalanche**: Pay minimums on all debts, throw excess at the highest interest rate.
- Pros: Mathematically optimal, minimizes interest paid, teaches rate-consciousness
- Cons: Payoff takes longer, no early wins, risk of burnout
- Best for: Discipline-strong households, high interest rate spread, large debt balances

**Hybrid**: Use avalanche on high-rate debt (>8%), snowball on low-rate debt (<5%), prioritize by impact.
- Pros: Combines speed (interest impact) with wins (small balances cleared)
- Cons: More complex to track, requires discipline
- Best for: Mixed debt portfolios (high-rate credit cards + low-rate mortgages)

**Decision tree**:
1. If total debt < 3x annual income → Snowball (momentum matters more)
2. If total debt > 3x annual income + rates vary >5% → Hybrid (avalanche on high-rate, snowball on low-rate)
3. If single debt or very high rate → Avalanche

### Cascade Quantification

The "cascade" is the monthly payment split across all debts. Example:

```
Total extra available: $500/month

Debt 1 (Target):    $400/month
Debt 2 (Minimum):   $ 75/month
Debt 3 (Minimum):   $ 25/month
```

Quantify:
- How much extra is flowing to the target debt?
- How much is "wasted" on minimums to non-target debts?
- If target is cleared, what's the new cascade? (Debt 2 becomes target)
- What's the payoff timeline at current cascade?

**Opportunity**: Could shifting income change which debt is target? (High-interest card vs. auto loan)

## Insurance Adequacy Framework

### Life Insurance Need

Formula: `(10-12x Annual Income) - (Existing Coverage) - (Liquid Assets) = Gap`

Example:
- Annual income: $100k
- Multiple: 10 (conservative)
- Existing coverage: $50k
- Liquid assets: $20k
- **Gap: (10 × $100k) - $50k - $20k = $930k needed**

### Disability Insurance Need

Rule of thumb: 60% of gross income for 3-6 months, then transition to government disability (if available).

### Property & Liability

- Home insurance: Replacement value (not actual cash value)
- Vehicle insurance: Full coverage if financed, at least liability if owned
- Umbrella: $1-2M excess liability, typically $150-300/year

## Emergency Fund Sizing

| Situation | Months of Expenses | Rationale |
|-----------|-------------------|-----------|
| Dual income, stable jobs | 3 months | Quick recovery possible |
| Single income, stable job | 6 months | Longer to find new role |
| Freelance/variable income | 9-12 months | Income gaps are normal |
| Business owner | 12 months | Business interruption risk |
| During debt payoff | 1-2 months | Can be tapped and rebuilt |

**Post-debt**: Immediately boost to 6-12 months and keep untouched.

## Investment Timing: Debt vs. Investing

Decision tree:

1. **If debt > 8% interest rate**: Pay debt first (after emergency fund and employer match)
   - Exception: If employer offers 50%+ match on retirement, take the match and accelerate debt with the paycheck extra

2. **If debt 5-8% interest rate**: This is the gray zone
   - If cash flow is tight: Pay debt
   - If cash flow is comfortable: Do both (debt minimums + some investing)

3. **If debt < 5% interest rate**: Investing becomes viable
   - Especially if mortgage/student loan at 3-4% (mathematical arbitrage)
   - But sequence matters: employer match first, then debt, then taxable investing

**Rule**: Don't hold high-yield savings (earning 4-5%) while paying credit card debt at 18-24%. The math doesn't work.

## Lifestyle Creep Prevention

Lifestyle creep kills plans. When income rises, the extra doesn't automatically flow to debt.

**Detection**:
- Does expense baseline rise whenever income rises?
- Does new income get "allocated" to new wants?
- Is the household earning more but not paying down debt faster?

**Prevention**:
1. **Budget the raise before you earn it** — Decide in advance where new income goes (e.g., 80% to debt, 20% to lifestyle)
2. **Automate the allocation** — Set up transfers on payday before the money hits checking
3. **Track the baseline** — Month over month, is core spending rising?
4. **Celebrate milestones, not purchases** — Debt cleared = celebration, not debt cleared + new car

**Wealth building rule** (Clason): "Pay yourself first" means debt/investing, not consumption.

## Post-Debt Wealth Building Priorities

Once household is debt-free:

### Immediate (Month 1)
- Boost emergency fund to 6-12 months expenses (if not already there)

### Months 2-6
1. Capture employer match on retirement accounts (free money)
2. Max out tax-advantaged retirement accounts (country-dependent: 401k, TFSA, ISA, superannuation)
3. Build taxable investing account

### Months 7+
- Once tax-advantaged accounts are maxed, invest in low-fee index funds
- Consider real estate or business investment (not before debt is cleared)

### Always
- Maintain emergency fund at 6-12 months (don't let it drop)
- Review insurance annually (may need adjustment as net worth grows)

## Key Psychological Principles

**From Housel (Psychology of Money)**:
- Patience is wealth-building's hidden advantage (time in market > timing market)
- Financial peace isn't about being rich; it's about options
- Small decisions compound over decades

**From Bach (Latte Factor)**:
- Small daily expenses ($5 coffee) compound: $5/day × 365 days × 40 years at 8% return = $530k
- Consciousness of small spending is the lever

**From Sethi (Conscious Spending)**:
- You need a system, not willpower
- Automate the "good" choices so you don't have to decide every day
- Conscious spending means intentional wants, not guilt-free guilt

**From Ramsey (Baby Steps)**:
- Gazelle intensity (extreme focus on one goal) beats gradual progress
- Debt is temporary; staying broke is optional

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

1. **Comparing to peers** — Someone else's timeline isn't yours; focus on the plan
2. **Deferring the plan for "perfect" circumstances** — Start now with what you have
3. **Using debt payoff as an excuse to starve** — Sustainable plans allow for life
4. **Switching methods mid-stream** — Pick snowball/avalanche and stick to it for at least 12 months
5. **Ignoring expense creep** — Small increases compound into broken plans
6. **Hoarding cash while paying debt** — High-interest debt + low-interest savings is mathematical loss
7. **No written plan** — Verbal plans fail; written plans succeed
