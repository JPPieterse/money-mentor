# Estate Planning Principles Reference

This document provides frameworks for wills, trusts, guardianship, life insurance, and digital estate. Combined with country-specific modules for legal requirements and tax treatment.

---

## Will Essentials by Country Type

**Note**: Estate planning laws vary dramatically. This is a framework; consult a local attorney for your jurisdiction.

### Common Law Countries (USA, UK, Canada, Australia, etc.)

**Will requirements**:
- Written document (handwritten or typed; some places accept digital)
- Clear testator intent (person making the will is mentally competent, not under duress)
- Signature (testator signs in front of witnesses)
- Witnesses (typically 2 unrelated adults; they sign and date)
- No formal probate requirement to make a will valid (though probate validates it for asset transfer)

**Probate process**:
- Will filed with court
- Court validates will (is it real? Is testator dead?)
- Executor named in will takes charge
- Assets transferred through court (can take 6 months to 2+ years)
- Creditors notified (debts paid before heirs get anything)
- Estate taxes paid
- Remaining assets distributed per will

**Timeline**: 1-2 years typically
**Cost**: $2,000-$5,000+ in legal and probate fees
**Downside**: Expensive, slow, public (will is public record)

### Civil Law Countries (Germany, France, Switzerland, etc.)

**Succession laws**:
- Intestate succession (who inherits if no will) is heavily prescribed by law
- Spouse typically gets 1/2 to 1/3, children get rest (order varies by country)
- Less flexibility than common law (can't disinherit children in some jurisdictions)
- Written will required (handwritten acceptable in some countries)
- Often requires notarization or official recording

**Probate process**: Similar but varies; consult local attorney.

## Guardianship Considerations

If the household has minor children, guardianship is critical.

### Naming a Guardian (in the will)

**What it does**: If both parents die, court appoints the guardian you named (vs. someone random).

**What it doesn't do**: Doesn't automatically give guardianship (court still must approve).

**How to choose**:
1. Values alignment: Will they raise kids the way you'd want?
2. Age and health: Will they be alive and capable in 15+ years?
3. Proximity: Will they keep kids in same school/community?
4. Capacity: Can they afford it? Do they have room?
5. Willingness: TALK TO THEM FIRST. Don't surprise anyone.

**Backup guardian**: Name a 2nd choice in case the 1st is unable to serve.

### Financial Guardian vs. Personal Guardian

Some jurisdictions allow splitting roles:
- **Personal guardian**: Raises the child day-to-day
- **Financial guardian**: Manages the child's money/inheritance

You could name a trusted relative to raise kids but a professional (accountant, attorney) to manage money.

### Testamentary Trust (for minor children)

Rather than giving children inheritance directly at age 18:
- Create a trust in your will
- Trustee manages the money until child reaches specified age (e.g., 25)
- Child can't blow inheritance at 18
- Trustee pays for education, living expenses, etc. as needed
- Child receives remainder at 25 (or whatever age you choose)

**Cost**: Minimal (built into will or standalone trust document)
**Benefit**: Protects inheritance from child's poor decisions or creditors

## Beneficiary Designation Checklist

Accounts with beneficiary designations bypass probate and go directly to the named beneficiary.

### Accounts That Typically Allow Beneficiary Designations

| Account Type | Beneficiary Allowed | Notes |
|--------------|-------------------|-------|
| Life insurance policy | Yes (primary + secondary) | Overrides will completely |
| 401(k) / IRA / TFSA | Yes | Each retirement account has separate designation |
| Bank savings account | Sometimes (varies by bank/country) | Ask your bank |
| Bank checking account | Sometimes | Ask your bank |
| Brokerage account | Sometimes | Ask your broker |
| Vehicle title | Sometimes (varies by state/country) | Ask your DMV/equivalent |
| Bonds (treasury, etc.) | Yes | Varies by type |

### Review Process

For each beneficiary account:
1. Find the account
2. Locate original beneficiary form
3. Verify: Primary beneficiary (who gets it first)
4. Verify: Secondary beneficiary (if primary is dead or declines)
5. Check: Form is signed, dated, witnessed (if required)
6. Update if: Relationships changed (divorce, new children, etc.)

### Common Mistakes

**Mistake 1**: Never updated beneficiary after divorce
- Divorced person dies, ex gets the life insurance (oops)
- Will says new spouse gets everything, but insurance went to ex
- New spouse gets angry, litigation ensues

**Mistake 2**: Named minor child directly as beneficiary
- Child gets insurance money at age 18 (or 21)
- May not have financial maturity
- Solution: Create trust in will, name trust as beneficiary, trustee manages money

**Mistake 3**: Named "estate" as beneficiary
- Insurance goes through probate (defeats purpose of beneficiary designation)
- Insurance is taxable to the estate (potentially higher taxes)
- Solution: Name individuals or trust, not the estate

**Mistake 4**: Multiple accounts with conflicting designations
- Life insurance names new spouse; retirement account names old spouse; bank account names children
- Chaos and litigation
- Solution: Audit all accounts and make designations consistent with your will

## Life Insurance Needs Analysis Formula

### Step 1: Calculate Target Coverage

```
Target = (Income Multiple × Annual Gross Income)
         - Existing Life Insurance
         - Liquid Assets (savings, investment accounts)
```

**Income multiple guidance**:
- Conservative (10x): Assumes surviving spouse will work, returns to work within 6 months
- Moderate (11x): Some career transition time, some lifestyle adjustment
- Generous (12x): Maximum security, account for grief-related job loss

**Example household**:
- Household income: $80,000/year
- Existing insurance: $100,000
- Liquid savings: $30,000
- Multiple: 10 (conservative, two-income household)

```
Target = (10 × $80,000) - $100,000 - $30,000
       = $800,000 - $100,000 - $30,000
       = $670,000 gap
```

### Step 2: Verify Coverage by Earner

If dual-earner household, calculate for each:
- **Primary earner**: 10-12x their salary
- **Secondary earner**: 7-10x their salary (accounts for lower loss if secondary is lost)

Example (dual-earner):
- Primary (earns $50,000): Need $500,000 coverage
- Secondary (earns $30,000): Need $210,000 coverage
- Total household need: $710,000

Check each person's coverage separately, then add.

### Step 3: Types of Coverage

| Type | Duration | Cost | Use Case |
|------|----------|------|----------|
| Term 10 | Covers 10 years | Cheapest | Young families, temporary need |
| Term 20 | Covers 20 years | Moderate | Family with young kids |
| Term 30 | Covers 30 years | Moderate | Family with older kids |
| Whole Life | Lifetime | Expensive | Wealthy households, estate planning |
| Universal Life | Flexible | Medium | Balance of cost and duration |

**Recommendation**: Term life insurance is typically best value for families. Covers the years of high financial need (kids in school, mortgage), then you'll be debt-free and able to self-insure.

### Storage Security

**Option 1**: Print and seal in envelopes, give to trusted family member
**Option 2**: Store in a password manager (1Password, LastPass, Bitwarden) with designated emergency contact
**Option 3**: Safe deposit box at bank (but access might be delayed during probate)
**Option 4**: Encrypted document with recovery person having the decryption key

**Rule**: Partner should know where this document is and how to access it.

## Key Principles for Planning

1. **Do it now.** No one thinks they'll die soon; accidents happen.
2. **Make it clear.** Ambiguous wills create litigation.
3. **Keep it updated.** Major life changes (marriage, kids, divorce) require updates.
4. **Store it safely.** Locked safe or attorney's office (not a drawer at home where it might burn in a house fire).
5. **Tell someone.** Partner should know where documents are and what you want.
6. **Consult an attorney.** Estate laws vary by jurisdiction. A $300 attorney consultation beats a $10,000 probate dispute.
