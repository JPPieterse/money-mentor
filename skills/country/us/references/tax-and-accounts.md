## 401(k) Plans: Traditional vs. Roth (2026)

### Contribution Limits

| Category | Amount |
|---|---|
| Employee deferral (age <50) | $24,500/year |
| Catch-up contribution (age 50+) | $8,000/year |
| **Total limit (age 50+)** | **$32,500/year** |
| Catch-up, ages 60–63 (SECURE 2.0) | $11,250/year instead of $8,000 |
| **Total limit (ages 60–63)** | **$35,750/year** |
| Overall annual additions cap (employee + employer, IRC 415(c)) | $72,000/year |
| Compensation cap for plan purposes (401(a)(17)) | $360,000/year |

The ages 60–63 catch-up is a real planning lever: it is roughly $3,250/year more than the standard
50+ catch-up, and it applies only in those four years.

### Traditional 401(k)

| Feature | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Tax treatment** | Pre-tax contributions (deductible); earnings tax-deferred |
| **Tax on withdrawal** | Fully taxable as ordinary income |
| **RMDs** | Required at age 73 |
| **Early withdrawal** | 10% penalty before 59.5 (exceptions exist) |
| **Loans** | Allowed in some plans (up to 50% of balance, max $50,000) |
| **Roth conversion** | Can convert to Roth (taxed as income in conversion year) |

### Roth 401(k)

| Feature | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Tax treatment** | After-tax contributions (not deductible); earnings tax-free |
| **Tax on withdrawal** | Tax-free (both contributions and growth) |
| **RMDs** | Required at age 73 (can convert to Roth IRA to avoid) |
| **Early withdrawal** | 10% penalty on earnings before 59.5 |
| **Income limits** | No income limits (unlike Roth IRA) |
| **Backdoor strategy** | Not available (cannot reconvert back to Traditional) |

### Employer Match (CRITICAL)

- **Always maximize employer match** — it is free money
- Typical match: 3–6% of salary
- Example: $100,000 salary, 3% match = $3,000/year employer contribution
- **If you contribute less than the match threshold:** You leave money on the table
- **Vesting schedule:** Note when match becomes yours (typically 3–6 years); vesting faster with newer employers

## IRA Rules: Traditional & Roth (2026)

### Contribution Limits

| Category | Amount |
|---|---|
| Annual contribution (age <50) | $7,500/year |
| Catch-up (age 50+) | $1,100/year |
| **Total (age 50+)** | **$8,600/year** |

### Traditional IRA

| Feature | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Tax deduction** | **Fully deductible** if no employer plan OR income below phase-out |
| **Phase-out range (Single, 2026)** | $81,000–$91,000 (if employer plan) |
| **Phase-out range (MFJ, 2026)** | $129,000–$149,000 (if employer plan) |
| **Tax on earnings** | Tax-deferred (taxed on withdrawal as ordinary income) |
| **RMDs** | Required at age 73 |
| **Early withdrawal** | 10% penalty before 59.5 (exceptions: education, first home up to $10k) |
| **Spousal IRA** | Non-working spouse can contribute even if earner has plan |

### Roth IRA

| Feature | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Tax deduction** | No deduction (after-tax contributions) |
| **Income phase-out (Single/HoH, 2026)** | $153,000–$168,000 |
| **Income phase-out (MFJ, 2026)** | $242,000–$252,000 |
| **Tax on earnings** | Tax-free (no tax on withdrawal) |
| **RMDs** | NOT required during your lifetime |
| **Early withdrawal** | Contributions (not earnings) can withdraw anytime tax/penalty-free |
| **5-year rule** | Earnings tax-free only if account open 5+ years AND withdrawal age 59.5+ |
| **Conversions** | Traditional → Roth conversion allowed; taxed as income in conversion year |

### Backdoor Roth (High Earners)

**Strategy:** If Roth IRA income limit exceeded, contribute to Traditional IRA (non-deductible), then convert to Roth

**Tax implication:**
- Conversion is taxable as ordinary income
- **Pro-rata rule:** If you have **any** pre-tax IRAs (traditional, SEP-IRA, SIMPLE IRA), the conversion is taxed pro-rata
- Example: $100,000 pre-tax IRA balance + $7,500 new Traditional IRA contribution + conversion = 70% of conversion taxed

**Steps:**
1. Contribute $7,500 to Traditional IRA (non-deductible)
2. Immediately convert to Roth IRA
3. File IRS Form 8606 (non-deductible IRA contributions)
4. Report conversion on tax return

**Caution:** Check for other pre-tax IRA balances before executing; unexpected tax bill if pro-rata rule applies

### Roth Ladder

**Strategy:** Convert Traditional IRA to Roth in tranches over several years to spread tax liability

**Use case:** Early retirement; access to funds before age 59.5

**Timeline:**
- Year 1: Convert $50,000 Traditional → Roth; pay tax on conversion
- Year 2: Convert another $50,000; wait 5 years for earnings to grow tax-free
- After 5 years: Can withdraw funds tax-free (subject to 59.5 rule for earnings)

## Health Savings Account (HSA) Rules (2026)

### Contribution Limits

| Coverage Level | 2026 Limit | Age 55+ Catch-up |
|---|---|---|
| Self-only | $4,400 | +$1,000 |
| Family | $8,750 | +$1,000 |

### Eligibility Requirements
- Must be enrolled in high-deductible health plan (HDHP)
  - HDHP definition (2026): Deductible ≥$1,700 (self-only) or $3,400 (family)
  - HDHP out-of-pocket maximum (2026): ≤$8,500 (self-only) or ≤$17,000 (family)
- Cannot be claimed as dependent (e.g., adult child on parent's return)
- Cannot have other health coverage (limited exceptions: vision, dental, workers comp)

### Triple Tax Advantage
1. **Contributions:** Deductible (reduce taxable income)
2. **Growth:** Tax-free investment growth
3. **Withdrawals:** Tax-free if used for qualified medical expenses

### Qualified Medical Expenses

**Covered:**
- Deductibles, copays, coinsurance
- Prescription drugs (FDA-approved)
- Medical equipment: crutches, wheelchairs, blood pressure monitors, thermometers
- Dental: Fillings, extractions, dentures, root canals, orthodontia
- Vision: Glasses, contacts, LASIK
- Mental health: Therapy, psychiatric care
- Medical transportation (mileage, parking)

**NOT covered:**
- Cosmetic procedures (e.g., hair removal, teeth whitening)
- Fitness memberships, vitamins (unless prescribed)
- Toiletries, cosmetics
- Health insurance premiums (exception: COBRA, Medicare premiums after 65)

### HSA as Retirement Account

- After age 65: Can withdraw for any reason (taxed as ordinary income if non-medical)
- Medical expenses: Always tax-free regardless of age
- **Strategy:** Invest HSA like retirement account; use other funds for current medical expenses; withdraw HSA for medical expenses in retirement (documentation required)
- **Advantage:** Triple tax benefit + flexibility in retirement

## Capital Gains Tax (2026)

### Long-Term Capital Gains (Assets held >1 year)

| Rate | Single Filer | Married Filing Jointly |
|---|---|---|
| 0% | Taxable income up to $49,450 | Up to $98,900 |
| 15% | $49,451–$545,500 | $98,901–$613,700 |
| 20% | Above $545,500 | Above $613,700 |

**Net Investment Income Tax:** an extra 3.8% applies to net investment income (including capital gains)
once modified AGI passes $200,000 (single) or $250,000 (married filing jointly). Those two thresholds are
set in statute and are **not** inflation-adjusted, so more people cross them every year.

### Short-Term Capital Gains (Assets held ≤1 year)

- Taxed as ordinary income (same as salary)
- Rates range 10%–37% depending on tax bracket

### Examples

**Example 1: Long-term gain (held 2+ years)**
- Buy stock for $10,000
- Sell for $15,000
- Gain = $5,000
- Tax at 15% LTCG rate = $750 (if in 15% bracket)

**Example 2: Short-term gain (held <1 year)**
- Buy stock for $10,000
- Sell for $15,000 after 6 months
- Gain = $5,000
- Tax at ordinary rate (if 22% bracket) = $1,100

**Example 3: Multiple gains**
- LTCG of $50,000 (taxed at 15% = $7,500)
- STCG of $20,000 (taxed at 22% = $4,400)
- Total tax = $11,900

### Net Capital Loss Deduction

- Can deduct up to $3,000 of net losses per year against ordinary income
- Excess losses carry forward to future years indefinitely

## Student Loan Interest Deduction (2026)

### Rules
- **Max deduction:** $2,500/year
- **Qualifications:** Loan must be for education expenses (tuition, fees, books)
- **Income phase-out (Single):** $85,000–$100,000
- **Income phase-out (MFJ):** $175,000–$205,000
- **Treatment:** "Above the line" deduction (reduces adjusted gross income)

### Example
- Student loan interest paid: $3,000/year
- Deductible amount: $2,500
- Tax savings at 22% bracket: $550

## Income Tax Withholding & Credits

### Earned Income Tax Credit (EITC)

**Purpose:** Tax credit for low-to-moderate income workers

- **2026 maximum credit: $8,231** for taxpayers with three or more qualifying children (up from $8,046
  in 2025).
- The per-category amounts and income limits (no children / 1 / 2 / 3+, and the separate married-filing-
  jointly thresholds) sit in a full table that changes every year. **Look them up rather than working
  from memory:** irs.gov → "EITC income limits and maximum credit amounts", or Rev. Proc. 2025-32.
- ⚠ *Not yet transcribed here — the earlier version of this file carried a table of 2021 figures for
  five years. A pointer that is right beats a table that is quietly wrong.*

### Child Tax Credit

- **$2,200 per qualifying child** (under age 17)
- Income phase-out: $400,000 (MFJ), $200,000 (single)
- Partially refundable as the Additional Child Tax Credit: up to $1,700 per child if you owe little or
  no tax

### Saver's Credit (Retirement Contribution Credit)

- Up to $1,000 credit for low-income retirement contributions ($2,000 married filing jointly)
- 2026 income limits: **$80,500** (MFJ), **$60,375** (head of household), **$40,250** (single)

## National Insurance: Self-Employment & Payroll Taxes

### Self-Employment Tax (2026)

- **Social Security tax:** 12.4% on net self-employment income (up to the $184,500 wage base)
- **Medicare tax:** 2.9% on all net self-employment income (no cap)
- **Additional Medicare tax:** 0.9% on self-employment income over $200,000 (single), $250,000 (MFJ).
  Not inflation-adjusted.
- **Deductible portion:** 50% of SE tax deductible on Form 1040
- **Quarterly estimated tax:** Required if you expect >$1,000 tax liability from self-employment

### Employee Payroll Taxes (2026)

| Tax | Rate | Max Wage Base (2026) |
|---|---|---|
| Social Security (employee) | 6.2% | $184,500 |
| Medicare (employee) | 1.45% | Unlimited |
| Additional Medicare | 0.9% (over threshold) | N/A |

The Social Security wage base rose from $176,100 in 2025 to **$184,500** in 2026, so a high earner pays
roughly $521 more in Social Security tax this year than last.

## Social Security Taxation

### Provisional Income Calculation

**Provisional income = Adjusted Gross Income + (1/2 of Social Security benefits) + Tax-exempt interest**

### How Much Is Taxable?

| Provisional Income (Single) | Provisional Income (MFJ) | Taxable Portion of Benefits |
|---|---|---|
| Up to $25,000 | Up to $32,000 | 0% |
| $25,001–$34,000 | $32,001–$44,000 | Up to 50% |
| Above $34,000 | Above $44,000 | Up to 85% |

These thresholds are written into statute and have **never** been inflation-adjusted, so each year a
larger share of retirees pay tax on their benefits. Do not expect them to move.

### Example
- Social Security benefit: $30,000/year
- Other income (salary, interest): $50,000
- Provisional income: $50,000 + $15,000 (1/2 benefit) = $65,000
- Above $34,000 threshold: Up to 85% of benefits taxable
- Estimated taxable benefit: ~$20,000
- Total taxable income: ~$70,000

## Filing & Deadlines

### Tax Return Due Date
- **Individual returns:** April 15 following tax year
- **Extension:** Form 4868 extends deadline to October 15 (no penalty if extension filed before April 15)
- **Estimated taxes:** Quarterly; due April 15, June 15, September 15, January 15

### Records to Keep
- Tax returns and supporting schedules (7 years minimum)
- W-2s, 1099s, K-1s
- Receipts for charitable donations, business expenses, medical expenses
- Mortgage interest statements, property tax bills
- Investment account statements (cost basis, dividends, interest)
